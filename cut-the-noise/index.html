01Last November

Last November felt like a turning point in my experience with AI coding tools. After months of experimentation and hesitation, the models began producing code I felt I could trust. That changed what we needed from verification.

02A reviewer we could trust

Earlier in 2025, our AI reviewer was so noisy that coworkers ran scripts to delete its comments. I asked Angie about a better model. As coding models improved, building a reviewer we could trust became more urgent: verification had to keep up with authorship.

03Cut the Noise

I’m Joah Gerstenberg, a tech lead at Block working on agents that can safely change production. Linters, tests, and compilers only take us so far. This talk covers what I’ve learned while building an intelligent reviewer that earns our trust.

04The old software factory

Writing and reviewing code used to pace the software factory. We knew our libraries closely, verified each other’s work, and learned from one another through review. Features took months to plan, build, and ship.

05Review is the new rate limit

Agents filled our review inboxes with changes, but we still had to decide whether those changes were safe for customers. Increasing code production did not remove the need for verification. Review became the new rate limit.

06Code reviewed per human

Code reviewed per human increased about 3× for the median reviewer at Block, measured directly across human- and bot-authored PRs. The illustrative chart is indexed to October 2025 at 1×. This measures review load per person, rather than total code sent to production.

07LGTM

Human attention is spread thin. Review increasingly becomes a ritual of trust: do I trust that my teammate is following our plan, and that their agents understand the bigger picture? A review step alone does not guarantee understanding.

08The code was never the goal

My background in UX engineering taught me that code is a means to a customer experience. The goal is not a TypeScript library; it is a customer buying a cappuccino without being double-charged. With agents writing code, understanding that experience matters even more.

Sources: Programming as Theory Building, 1985

09We are still responsible

We want to deliver an experience we are willing to sign our names to. When an agent gets it wrong, pointing at the agent does not help the customer. We still hold responsibility for its output and need systems that protect our intended experience.

10Calibrate your reviewer to past human reviews

We compare reviewer behavior with manually labeled examples from subject matter experts. Hundreds of historical reviews give us known issues to test against. They help measure whether the reviewer catches those issues, while new findings still need their own assessment.

Sources: DoorDash Engineering Blog · 2026

11The cost of noise

Our reviewer once posted a comment that merely restated the configuration line it was reviewing. The author called it the worst comment they had ever seen. Feedback like this prompted us to shut the reviewer down and rebuild it.

Block internal data

12A reviewer that’s muted catches nothing

We learned to set a high bar for precision. When people cannot trust the findings, they stop listening, and valid findings disappear into the noise too. The coworkers who deleted the bot’s comments were responding to a real problem.

Sources: DoorDash Engineering Blog · 2026 · Figma Blog · 2026

13Offline and online evals

Offline evaluations use labeled examples to measure precision and recall. Online evaluations show how the reviewer behaves on real production work: whether findings are acted on and whether recommended changes later need correction or reversion. Both are needed to understand the system.

14Prioritize precision over recall

We have swung between trying to catch every issue and making sure reported issues are legitimate. For comments placed in front of people, precision comes first. Recall still matters, but preserving attention gives valid findings a chance to be heard.

Source: OpenAI Alignment Blog · 2025

15ReviewBench

Our latest grounded ReviewBench results are 88.0% precision, 37.8% recall, and 52.9% F1. F1 combines precision and recall using their harmonic mean. The comparison shows room to improve coverage across reviewers, which do not necessarily miss the same issues.

Sources: ReviewBench

16Online evals: address rate

Address rate tracks the share of findings that led to a code change. It helps us understand whether review is useful in the workflow. It does not prove that every addressed finding was a real bug or tell us which issues the reviewer missed.

Block internal data

17Different reviewers find different issues

Our reviewer and another reviewer overlapped on only 22% of findings, using the larger finding count as the denominator. They found substantially different sets of issues. Different models, prompts, and specialties can offer different perspectives on the same change.

Block internal data

18Three specialists

In our evaluation, three specialists increased recall from 41% to 52%, an improvement of 11 percentage points. The specialists focus on money movement, security, and correctness. Broader coverage also requires controlling cost and filtering their combined output.

Block internal data

19Risk-based review tiering

Review depth should match the risk of the change. Routine changes can take a lighter path, while changes with greater consequences receive deeper review. Meta’s RADAR provides an example of combining eligibility checks, automated review, and validation for low-risk changes.

Source: Meta · RADAR · 2026

20Reviewers, coordinator, judge, human

Specialists report findings to a coordinator that deduplicates and filters them. A judge challenges the evidence and revisits dismissals, with sensitive or unresolved questions handed to a human. More perspectives can improve recall; stronger validation can improve precision, and both need measurement.

Sources: DoorDash Engineering Blog · 2026 · Figma Blog · 2026

21Where human attention lands

Human judgment belongs on sensitive changes and questions automation cannot confidently resolve. Depending on the system, that includes infrastructure, money movement, personal information, and decisions requiring context. Bring humans the evidence and unresolved question so they can focus on what matters.

22What’s my job now?

If we no longer read every line, we still need to decide what our systems may do, what they need to understand, and how we will detect mistakes. Alongside evaluations, that means containing and aligning our agents.

23Contain and align

Containment sets the boundaries of autonomy and determines when deeper review or a human decision is needed. Alignment gives agents the intended customer experience and constraints, such as charging once for one coffee. We maintain those boundaries and that context as the system changes.

24Give your agents the big picture

AGENTS.md, skills, tools, and shared context can explain why the system exists and what successful behavior means. Go beyond coding conventions to describe the customer experience and the things that must remain true. Authors and reviewers both need that understanding.

25Get review before the code is written

Review the approach while it is still easy to change direction. Humans and agents should agree on the experience, constraints, and evidence of success before implementation begins. Plans and tools such as Buzz can help carry that shared understanding into the work.

Sources: Buzz

26Cut the noise

Prioritize precision to protect attention, give agents the context to recognize the bugs that matter, and review plans before code is written. Use different perspectives and review depth where the risk calls for them. Keep measuring whether the system earns trust.

27A workflow we can trust

Agents keep the factory moving while the review system checks their work. Humans remain responsible for the customer experience, important decisions, and shared context. The aim is to take responsibility without manually reading every line of code.
