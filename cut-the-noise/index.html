01November 12, 2025

Our reviewer was so noisy that engineers wrote scripts to delete its comments and teams asked to disable it. I asked Angie about a better model and DM’d our CTO. With more powerful coding models arriving, the question became how to build a reviewer that earns the right to interrupt us.

02Cut the Noise

The old software factories are in trouble. Work that used to take weeks or months is now a few keystrokes away. Our focus is shifting toward understanding the customer experience, balancing agent speed with production reliability, and building agentic systems we can trust.

03The old bottleneck

Not long ago, writing code paced the factory, and most teams expected thorough human review of agent-written changes. Reviewing each other’s code also taught new teammates how our systems worked. But the models keep getting better.

04The factory speeds up

With agents at the helm, writing code is no longer the bottleneck. Our assumptions about how work moves through the factory are breaking: code arrives faster, while the human reviewer’s capacity has not grown along with it.

05Review load

More PRs arrive at each active reviewer, and the changes themselves are larger. Our illustrative workload estimate combines those trends. This is why writing code faster creates a new bottleneck in the attention required to understand and review it.

06Cognitive surrender

The person at the end of the line has not gained the capacity to inspect the pile. When the output overwhelms our attention, we can disengage and approve without really understanding the change.

07A little about me

I’m Joah Gerstenberg, a tech lead at Block focused on risk-based agent autonomy. I spent the first decade of my career building web experiences, which shaped how I think about the purpose of our code.

08We still own the experience

When a customer brings back a broken experience, pointing to the agent does not solve their problem. We are still responsible for what we deliver, and we have to be able to hold that responsibility.

09The program is bigger than the code

The customer buying coffee is the point of all this machinery. The program includes our understanding of the problem, the intended experience, and the reasons behind our choices. That theory lives in its creators’ minds, and the code does not capture all of it.

Source: Programming as Theory Building, 1985

10Offline and online evals

Offline evaluations measure precision—the share of findings that are valid—and recall—the share of known defects found. Online evaluations measure address rate, whether engineers act on findings, and change fail rate, the share of merged changes later reverted or corrected in a follow-up commit. Use a consistent follow-up window and count each failed change once.

11Calibrate against human judgment

Trust starts with evaluations. Past expert reviews give us examples of what good review looks like in our application. Preserve the judgments and their context, include clean changes and known failures, and use that corpus to calibrate the reviewer.

13The cost of noise

We rolled out a change that dramatically decreased precision, and our online address rate plummeted. This comment simply restates the code. Engineers started ignoring our reviews again, so we shut automatic reviews off. That experience leads directly to the high bar for precision on the next slide.

14A reviewer that’s muted catches nothing

DoorDash prioritizes precision, challenges findings before posting, and measures whether engineers keep the reviewer enabled and act on comments. Figma also withheld PR comments until its reviewer met a sustained precision threshold. Both treat a high bar for findings as a condition for earning engineers’ attention.

17Build a circle of trust

Use reviewers with clear specializations: money movement, security, correctness, and validation. They ask different questions about the same change. A mix of specializations and models can give us more useful perspectives than one general reviewer alone. Dispatching the full panel on every review would be expensive, so the next step is choosing when each perspective is needed. In an offline evaluation on 100 historical PRs using the same model, three specialists found 52% of known bugs compared with 41% for one reviewer. This gain is recall; the available partial diagnostics also showed lower precision, reinforcing the need to challenge and deduplicate their findings.

18Match review to risk

Don’t send every review to the full panel. Assess the change’s risk signals and reserve the most expensive reviews for the highest-risk changes. Routine work gets a smaller review; money movement or a security boundary brings in specialists and a different policy for human attention.

19Challenge findings before escalating

Specialist reviewers look for specific classes of issues and bubble up findings to a coordinator that deduplicates overlap and challenges weak evidence. An adversarial judge rechecks the change and candidates before escalation. To search for missed defects, it needs the underlying change and context; validating a list alone cannot establish recall. More perspectives broaden coverage, while validation layers improve precision. Our disprove filter cut the number of comments by 35%; the point is to remove weak findings before asking a human to spend attention on them.

Sources: DoorDash · Figma

20Reserve human attention

Reserve human review for the most sensitive changes: infrastructure, judgment-sensitive behavior, money movement, and personally identifying information. These signals should reflect the application. An agent’s silence does not remove the need for human judgment.

21What is my job now?

If agents write code and help review it, what is the software engineer’s job? The responsibility shifts toward understanding the experience, setting boundaries, and deciding where human judgment is still essential.

22Contain and align

Contain agents’ impact by choosing the risks we guard against, the boundaries they operate within, and the changes that need extra attention. Align their work with the intent of the application so the factory delivers the experience we actually want.

23Give agents the big picture

AGENTS.md, skills, and tools carry context to our agents. Humans maintain the intended behavior, constraints, application understanding, and knowledge behind those mechanisms. Reviewers need that bigger picture to understand what a change is trying to accomplish.

24Review before implementation

Work in the open and invite teammates into agent sessions. In Buzz, humans and agents collaborate continuously on plans and intent. Getting review before code is written moves judgment earlier, while the implementation is still easy to change.

Source: Buzz, buzz.xyz

25Cut the noise

Prioritize precision, calibrate against past human reviews, and measure performance in real work. Match review depth to risk and reserve human judgment for sensitive changes. Give agents the customer experience and intent behind the code, and get review while the plan is still easy to change.

26Thank you

Build the context, tune the risk signals, and collaborate on intent before implementation. Agents can do more of the mechanical work while humans keep responsibility for the program and the experience it creates.
